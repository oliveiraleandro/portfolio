CAP — Redesigning a real estate platform

A clearer, more consistent experience for real estate agents on desktop and mobile.

  • B2B Systems
  • Information Architecture
  • Responsive Design
  • Data Visualization

Context and challenge

CAP is a B2B platform that brings together properties, listings, owners, price history, and market data. It supports real estate agents in researching, sourcing, tracking, and pricing properties.

When I joined the project, the product needed a complete redesign. The client felt its appearance did not suit a professional audience, and the structure of large tables, tabs, and modals made it difficult to use on mobile.

There was one important condition: no features could be removed. I could reorganize information and change interactions, but I needed to preserve all existing capabilities.

My role

I had worked with the client before and was invited to redesign part of another product for real estate agency managers. That work was well received and led to the complete redesign of CAP.

For approximately three months, I was the project’s only designer. I worked directly with the founder and developers, who had extensive real estate experience. There was no formal product manager or product owner, and the founder was actively involved in decisions.

My work included mapping the platform, reviewing other products for reference, talking with real estate agents, redesigning user journeys, and organizing components for implementation.

Getting to know the product

With access to the development environment, I explored the screens and inventoried content, features, and navigation paths. Because the scope covered the entire product, this assessment was essential to reorganizing it without leaving any capabilities behind.

I found three main problems:

  • A structure poorly suited to mobile: wide tables and flows with many levels would be difficult to use on small screens.
  • An underused home page: the page functioned almost like a menu, with little information about work in progress.
  • Inconsistent visuals: typefaces, icons, colors, and illustrations varied across areas.

This combination contributed to an appearance the client perceived as childish. The redesign needed a more cohesive visual language and an organization suited to professional workflows.

Product references and context of use

Because many B2B real estate platforms were closed to public access, I expanded my research to products such as ZAP, QuintoAndar, and Zillow, as well as Captei, which was closer to CAP’s context. I looked at search, listings, information density, and property presentation.

I kept some colors from the previous identity but reduced the number used on each screen. Illustrations gave way to photography, simple backgrounds, and subtle gradients. I also established a smaller, more consistent type scale.

I talked with two real estate agents to understand their work outside the office. They confirmed that they spent many hours on the road on showing days, reinforcing the importance of a mobile version that was useful on the job.

The conversations also surfaced difficulties with pricing and lead qualification. CAP’s role was to provide better information to support the agent’s judgment. This definition helped keep proposals within the product’s scope.

A home page for picking up where you left off

I proposed bringing recent sourcing activity and recent follow-ups to the home page. Sourcing referred to properties the agent was looking to add to their portfolio; follow-ups represented deals in progress.

The idea emerged from journey mapping as a design hypothesis. By showing current activity, the home page could help agents resume their work with less navigation.

It therefore brought together access to modules and ways to continue ongoing work. I still see room to develop this direction further and bring the entry point closer to the most frequent tasks.

Finding the visual direction

I started on desktop with simple sketches and quickly moved to detailed, interactive prototypes. The founder preferred exploring proposals independently, and this format supported that evaluation.

The initial direction was validated with the client and a real estate agent. I then consolidated the visual patterns and developed the mobile versions.

This sequence helped establish the product’s visual language before formalizing the component system. The resulting foundation supported both mobile adaptations and the handoff to developers.

From tables to cards on mobile

On desktop, I kept the property list as a table, useful for comparing, sorting, and reviewing many data points at once. On mobile, I turned each row into a card to avoid scrolling in two directions.

The change also required rethinking the hierarchy. In the table, recent visits appeared in the last column. In the card, they moved to the top because they served as an indirect signal of activity around the property.

I used the secondary color and a heavier font weight for this information. Other details received a more compact presentation, with access to the full details on the next screen.

The card followed the sequence recent activity → property summary → primary action. At the bottom, a primary-color button filled the width. The adaptation preserved the content while reorganizing how it was read in the new space.

Property details: a negotiated solution

The detail view was the most challenging screen in the project. It combined a large amount of information across tabs and modals. My first mobile proposal organized the content into a sequence without tabs, but the client preferred keeping the existing structure.

The alternative also had a drawback: it created a very long page. I kept the tabs and refined their behavior on mobile, making active and inactive states more distinct and keeping every tab accessible without additional horizontal scrolling.

This compromise preserved the requested navigation while reducing some of the difficulties on small screens.

Pricing to support decisions

The pricing area already contained important information, but it was presented mainly in tables. I reorganized the presentation to make it easier to read and interpret.

Different pricing concepts received their own colors, icons, and visual treatments. Current values became more prominent, while historical data appeared as a time series with trend and change indicators. I also created comparisons with nearby properties.

This was one of the areas the client responded to most positively. During validation, the real estate agent showed interest in the history and trends but also asked about data accuracy.

The feedback showed how a clearer presentation raises expectations of trustworthy information. I asked the client about missing or uncertain data states and was told that uncertain data would not be displayed. Information quality remained dependent on the business and technology teams.

Business requirements and visual density

During validation, I learned that the header needed to keep owner and property limits visible because they were tied to the billing model. I also added a counter to make the number of properties being tracked more visible.

These requirements were incorporated into the screen hierarchy. On desktop, I retained relatively high information density, using groupings, cards, dividers, and typography to organize the content.

On mobile, the same content needed a different order and emphasis. This approach preserved functionality while adapting the experience to each context.

Validation and implementation

The client reviewed the interactive prototypes at their own pace. I also presented part of the work to an external real estate agent who did not use CAP. He found the navigation and presentation professional, but also emphasized his concern about data reliability.

After visual validation, I organized components, variants, and reusable patterns. The handoff took place in Figma, without a formal handoff meeting. The team’s front-end experience helped them reproduce the interfaces, and the parts I reviewed closely matched the prototype.

Results and lessons learned

The redesign covered the entire platform, established a consistent visual language, and reorganized information important to agents’ work. The mobile version received tailored solutions, especially for property lists and details.

There was no structured measurement phase. I suggested tracking metrics, but that did not move forward at the time and was outside the agreed budget and scope. The observed results were qualitative, with the pricing area receiving particularly positive feedback.

That area captures my contribution well: making existing data more understandable and useful for decisions. With more time, I would develop the home page further and observe usage after implementation to understand which changes improved agents’ daily work.
